How to Cycle a New Aquarium

Priya Anand Sep 2, 2026 7 min read

ON THIS PAGE

What Is Cycling? Why It Matters The Fishless Method Testing Along the Way Signs You're Done Common Mistakes

Every new aquarium needs time before it can safely support fish — not because the water looks wrong, but because of something invisible: a colony of beneficial bacteria that hasn't grown in yet.

What Is Cycling?

Cycling is the process of growing enough beneficial bacteria in your filter and substrate to process fish waste. Those bacteria convert ammonia (toxic) into nitrite (also toxic), and then into nitrate (comparatively safe, removed with regular water changes).

In an established tank this happens automatically in the background. In a brand-new tank, that bacterial colony doesn't exist yet — which is exactly why cycling matters before you add livestock.

In short: cycling turns a tank of plain water into an environment that can actually process what a fish produces.

Why It Matters

Adding fish to an uncycled tank is the single most common reason new aquariums fail. Ammonia builds up quickly, stresses fish, and can be fatal within days — a pattern experienced hobbyists call "new tank syndrome." A properly cycled tank avoids this entirely.

The Fishless Method

We recommend fishless cycling — dosing pure ammonia (or fish food, which breaks down into ammonia) rather than using live fish to "seed" the tank.

  • Add a small, measured dose of ammonia to reach roughly 2–4 ppm.
  • Test daily for ammonia and nitrite using a liquid test kit.
  • Once ammonia drops to zero within 24 hours, dose again and keep testing.
  • When both ammonia and nitrite read zero within 24 hours of dosing, and nitrate is present, the cycle is established.

This typically takes four to six weeks, though a filter seeded with mature media or substrate from an established tank can shorten that considerably.

Testing Along the Way

A liquid test kit (not strips) gives the most reliable readings for ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and pH. Test every one to two days during the cycle, and keep a simple log — the trend matters more than any single reading.

Signs You're Done

Your tank is cycled when, after dosing ammonia to 2–4 ppm, both ammonia and nitrite read zero within 24 hours and nitrate has climbed to a measurable level. At that point, do a large water change to bring nitrate down before adding your first fish.

Common Mistakes

Rushing the process is the most common mistake — adding fish "just to speed things up" almost always backfires. Overcleaning a new filter, using tap water conditioner inconsistently, and skipping regular testing are close behind. Patience here saves livestock later.
